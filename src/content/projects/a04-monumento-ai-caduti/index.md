  - images:
      - src: ./pages/page-06.jpg
    text: "The city of Milan commissioned this tower monument and its commemorative crypt to honor the ten thousand people who died in the First World War. The design is built on allegory and symbolism, starting with the site itself: land between the Catholic University and the Basilica of Sant'Ambrogio once used as a cemetery for early Christian martyrs, a history reflected in the building's octagonal plan. Each facade faces one of the city's eight gates, the same gates through which the fallen once left Milan. The four main facades each represent a year of the war, and the four diagonal ones represent earth, air, water, and fire. Every niche between the pillars holds soil from a different battlefield.

      The building also draws on older architecture: the atrium's proportions echo the Basilica of Sant'Ambrogio, the upper section (wrapped in a frieze by sculptor Salvatore Saponaro) recalls the Tower of the Winds in Athens, and the double spiral staircase is modeled on Sangallo's staircase at the fortress of Orvieto. Even the heavy white Musso marble continues a long tradition, one that runs from the Roman columns of San Lorenzo to the Milan Cathedral."
    smallText: true
  - images:
      - src: ./pages/page-07.jpg
    text: "With its strict, classical style and simple geometry, the building is plainer than the earlier competition entries that came out of academic tradition. It leans fully into that tradition to express historical continuity and a sense of eternity — yet it still embraces the modern, using reinforced concrete as a building material and adding a lighthouse above the so-called Hall of Eternal Light, meant to shine a beam of electric light over the city on days of remembrance.

      The War Memorial gave Muzio and Milan's twentieth-century architects their first real chance to collaborate with artists on a shared project, continuing a partnership pursued since the postwar period. Saponaro worked closely with the architects; A. Wildt created the great sculpture of Sant'Ambrogio, the city's patron saint, while other artists — G. Castiglioni, L. Andreotti, F. Lombardi, I. Griselli, A. Maiocchi, and G. Supino — contributed reliefs, sculptural groups, and railings. Writers and critics including P. Buzzi, U. Ojetti, and M. Sarfatti helped bring attention to the monument's inauguration.

      The monument suffered heavy damage in the 1943 bombing of Milan. It was rebuilt after the war and expanded in 1973 with the Shrine, a three-level underground addition."

We suggest an immediate removal of the existing silicone waterproofing and repairing the gaps with 'mastice da marmista,' a glue composed of resins dissolved in turpentine. In modern days, synthetic resin is applicable to many marble surfaces; the marble putty is made with polyester resin and can be colored by end users to match the original masonry materials."
